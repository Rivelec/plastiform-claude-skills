# Sources: what sales and marketing writers say about writing

Research done 2026-09-28 for the gemini-writer skill. Scope: how a text is written (words, sentences, paragraphs, tone, editing), not what it says. Rules about strategy, targeting, SEO, funnels or persuasion frameworks were left out.

Each entry gives the primary source read, then 3 to 6 rules in my own words. Text in quotation marks is a quote of one sentence at most, copied from the source. "Not verified" means I could not read it in a primary source.

Tags used in the last two sections: [Ogilvy] [Zinsser] [Handley] [Graham] [Fried] [Dry] [Wiebe] [Braun] [NNg] [Welsh] [Data] [Canivet] [Hardy] [Niederhoffer].

## 1. David Ogilvy: "How to Write" memo

- Source: internal memo to all Ogilvy & Mather staff, dated 7 September 1982 (Boing Boing's copy says 1981), published in *The Unpublished David Ogilvy* (1986). Full text reproduced at https://boingboing.net/2015/04/23/david-ogilvys-1982-memo.html (read) and https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/02/07/david-ogilvy-on-writing/.
- Rules:
  - Write the way you speak: "Write the way you talk."
  - "Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs."
  - No pretentious jargon (his examples: *reconceptualize*, *demassification*, *attitudinally*); he calls such words "hallmarks of a pretentious ass".
  - Don't send on the day you write. Read it aloud the next morning, then edit.
  - Before sending, make it "crystal clear what you want the recipient to do".
  - Keep it short: never more than two pages on any subject.

## 2. William Zinsser: *On Writing Well* and "Writing English as a Second Language"

- Sources: *On Writing Well* (1st ed. 1976), chapters 2 to 4 ("Simplicity", "Clutter", "Style") from the 1998 Harper edition, hosted by Georgia Tech: https://c14750.wpmucdn.com/359/files/2016/06/Zinsser.pdf (read). Talk given at Columbia Journalism School, published in *The American Scholar*, 1 December 2009: https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/ (read).
- Rules:
  - Strip every sentence down: cut words that do no work, long words where a short one exists, adverbs that repeat the verb, and passives that hide who does what. "The secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components."
  - Use the short word, not the long phrase ("now", not "at the present time"). Don't inflate job titles or things: his example is a salesman renamed "marketing representative".
  - Cut small qualifiers ("a bit", "sort of"), and cut sentences that repeat the one before.
  - One thought per sentence. Use plain nouns and active verbs rather than abstract Latin nouns. His rewrite: "Prior to the implementation of the financial enhancement" becomes "Before we fixed our money problems".
  - Be yourself and write in the first person. "He wants the person who is talking to him to sound genuine."
  - He names business letters, memos and corporate reports as the worst sources of clutter.

## 3. Ann Handley: *Everybody Writes*

- Sources: *Everybody Writes* (Wiley 2014, 2nd ed. 2022). "13 Writing Rules", annhandley.com, 24 August 2014: https://annhandley.com/13-writing-rules/ (read). Interview at the book launch, TopRank, 2014: https://www.toprankmarketing.com/blog/everybody-writes-ann-handley/ (read). "Why Care About The Stupid Em Dash Anyway?", 2026 (no date on the page): https://annhandley.com/why-care-about-the-em-dash/ (read). "9 Ingredients for an Email Readers Want to Open" (undated): https://annhandley.com/how-to-write-email-readers-want-to-open/ (read).
- Rules:
  - "No one will ever complain that you've made things too simple to understand."
  - Assume the reader knows nothing about the topic, but don't assume the reader is stupid.
  - Buzzwords and jargon are "the chemical additives of writing". Say "you" and "us", not "our customers" or "the organization".
  - She sees wordiness as the most common fault. Write an ugly first draft, then edit it for clarity for the reader, not just for grammar.
  - In emails and newsletters, start with a real moment (something you noticed or got wrong), not a topic. Add specific, slightly odd details, and cut anything that could have come from any other sender.
  - She defends the em dash: it is fine when it marks a real turn in the sentence. Readers take it as a machine sign only when it adds drama the sentence has not earned. See the disagreements section.

## 4. Paul Graham: three essays on writing

- Sources: "Writing, Briefly", March 2005: https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html. "Write Like You Talk", October 2015: https://paulgraham.com/talk.html. "Write Simply", March 2021: https://paulgraham.com/simply.html. All three read.
- Rules:
  - Test every sentence by asking "Is this the way I'd say this if I were talking to a friend?" If not, rewrite it the way you would say it. "If you simply manage to write in spoken language, you'll be ahead of 95% of writers."
  - Use ordinary words and simple sentences. A rare word or complex sentence is fine for effect, but "you should never do it by accident".
  - Don't try to sound impressive. Fancy prose makes the reader work harder just so the writer looks clever.
  - Cut everything that isn't needed. Most editing is cutting.
  - Read the text aloud to find awkward phrases and slow parts.
  - Write for a reader who reads less carefully than you. When you are pressed for time, write the most important sentence first.

## 5. Jason Fried (37signals)

- Sources: "Why Is Business Writing So Awful?", *Inc.*, 1 May 2010, read from the archived copy: https://web.archive.org/web/20130114175231/http://www.inc.com/magazine/20100501/why-is-business-writing-so-awful.html. *Getting Real* (37signals, 2006), chapter "Copywriting is Interface Design": https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/09.7-copywriting-is-interface-design (read).
- Rules:
  - Drop stock business phrases. His examples are "full-service solutions provider", "cost effective end-to-end solutions" and "provider of value-added services". If you write like everyone else, you are telling readers your product is like everyone else's.
  - Write to be read, not to fill space. He praises companies that "write to be read".
  - Describe the product in concrete detail and with a point of view. His model is Saddleback Leather explaining its leather, seams and 100-year warranty.
  - Use the customer's language: no internal terms, and no acronyms most people don't know. "Don't sound like an engineer talking to another engineer."
  - "Keep it short and sweet." Every word counts, down to a button label or the order of words in a notice.
  - Not verified: the "Sound like you" chapter of *Rework* (2010), summarized elsewhere as "write like you talk, friendly without being formal". I could only read summaries.

## 6. Harry Dry (Marketing Examples)

- Sources: marketingexamples.com, pages undated: "17 tips for great copywriting" https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/tips, "My guide to conversational copywriting" https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/conversational, and "7 practical ways to write copy that converts" https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/conversion (all read). The "three rules" come from his interview on David Perell's *How I Write*, 17 July 2024, as summarized by Perell's team at https://writingexamples.com/article/harry-dry-write-great-copy and clipped at https://x.com/PerellClips/status/2069915528328609873. I did not find them on a page Dry wrote himself, and his own `/copywriting/three-rules` page returned an error.
- Rules:
  - Test each claim three ways: "Can I visualize it? Can I falsify it? Can nobody else say it?" A sentence any competitor could sign fails the third test.
  - Don't exaggerate: "An honest line always feels warmer."
  - Kill adverbs and adjectives, and avoid "landing page words" (unlock, unleash, enhance, empower, supercharge). "Real people don't use them."
  - "More periods, fewer commas."
  - Write how you talk, with plenty of pronouns, to one reader. Use your customers' words, and don't reach for the thesaurus.
  - Read it aloud at the kitchen table. If someone cringes, rewrite it.
  - Doesn't transfer to French B2B: "Don't worry about grammar", starting sentences with conjunctions, and contractions. These are tied to informal English marketing copy.

## 7. Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers)

- Sources: *The 7 Sweeps* video tutorials she presented in November 2017: clarity, 7 Nov (https://copyhackers.com/how-to-edit-for-clarity/, page has no transcript); voice and tone, 14 Nov (https://copyhackers.com/voice-and-tone/); So What and Prove It, 21 Nov (https://copyhackers.com/edit-believability-sweeps-3-4/). Wistia interview, 2 March 2017: https://wistia.com/learn/marketing/joanna-wiebe-conversion-copywriting. The one-line definition of each sweep is from a Copyhackers staff article (Chris Silvestri, undated): https://copyhackers.com/ai-prompt/how-to-use-chatgpt-to-edit/. All read.
- Rules:
  - Edit in separate passes, each asking one question: clarity, voice and tone, so what, prove it, specificity, emotion, risk.
  - Voice pass: read line by line and ask whether each line fits the voice. Then read from the end back to the start, looking for lines with too much voice.
  - So-what pass: every claim must say why the reader should care. Her example: "help your team" leaves open *so that what?*
  - Prove-it pass: every claim needs proof of the same kind (a figure, a demo, a screenshot). For our writer this means: don't make a claim the facts can't back.
  - Specificity pass: "12% more sales in less than 6 months" beats "more sales", and filler like "really" and "very" goes. This definition is from the staff article, not from Wiebe herself.
  - Vary sentence length, and use phrasings customers have actually said (from the Wistia interview).

## 8. Josh Braun: cold email

- Sources: "15 Cold Email Copywriting Principles", PDF on joshbraun.com, 2021: https://joshbraun.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/15coldemail-copywriting.pdf (read in full). "Cold Email Systems Check", PDF, 2023: https://joshbraun.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Copy-of-Cold-Email-System-Check-V4.pdf (read). LinkedIn post, 29 July 2023: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/josh-braun_heres-one-piece-of-popular-cold-email-advice-activity-7091164011223732225-ePz9 (read).
- Rules:
  - "Don't write like you talk. Write how your customers' talk." His test: "Would a customer say this?" Words the prospect would use "over coffee". Jargon is fine when the audience itself uses it.
  - Delete jargon. His examples: "Flexible, scalable, groundbreaking" and "Optimize", "Streamline", "Cutting-Edge".
  - Cut fluff: useless words, "that", and "just" (you don't have to apologize for doing your job). His example: "A number of retail companies" becomes "Several retailers". Wait a minute, then reread twice before sending.
  - Be specific: "When you're specific you're more believable."
  - Length: "Nobody wants to read War & Peace." His checklist target is 74 to 100 words.
  - Keep the tone relaxed and low-pressure. End on a yes/no question such as "Worth exploring?" rather than asking for 30 minutes. No "just checking in" and no "Did you get my email?". This is close to strategy; I kept it for the phrasing.

## 9. Jakob Nielsen and John Morkes (Nielsen Norman Group): web writing study

- Source: "Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web", 1997: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/ (read).
- Findings and rules:
  - In their test, measured usability was 58% higher for the concise version of a page, 47% higher for the scannable version, 27% higher for the objective version, and 124% higher for all three combined.
  - Write objectively, not promotionally. Users "detest anything that seems like marketing fluff or overly hyped language ("marketese") and prefer factual information."
  - Make pages scannable: headings, lists, highlighted keywords, one idea per paragraph opened by its topic sentence.
  - Use the inverted pyramid: conclusion first, details after.

## 10. Justin Welsh: LinkedIn posts

- Sources: LinkedIn post "10 of the most useful LinkedIn writing tips", 14 July 2021 (date decoded from the post ID): https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinwelsh_10-of-the-most-useful-linkedin-writing-tips-activity-6821049129494450176-FiGM (read). Newsletter "My Writing Process for 162.597M+ Impressions On LinkedIn", 8 October 2022: https://www.justinwelsh.me/newsletter/my-writing-process-for-162-597m-impressions-on-linkedin (read). I kept only the writing tips and dropped his growth advice (niche, posting volume).
- Rules:
  - Write for mobile: short chunks, white space, shorter sentences.
  - Use simple words ("Nobody wants to grab a thesaurus") and write like you speak.
  - Address the reader as "you" and "your".
  - Cut 30 to 40% of the draft.
  - "Don't break lines in the middle. Write better lines." In other words, no artificial one-phrase-per-line layout.
  - Keep the first line short and easy to take in. Order: hook, then content, then a short recap.

## 11. Sales email data: Boomerang, Lavender, Gong

- Sources, all read:
  - Boomerang, "7 Tips for Getting More Responses to Your Emails (With Data!)", Alex Moore, 12 February 2016, 40 million emails from Boomerang users (all kinds of email, not only sales): https://blog.boomerangapp.com/2016/02/7-tips-for-getting-more-responses-to-your-emails-with-data/
  - Lavender:
    - Will Allred, "Data says: The Shorter, the Better for Your Cold Email", 8 January 2023: https://lavender.ai/blog/best-length-cold-email
    - Will Allred, "The One-Idea Rule", 10 May 2023: https://lavender.ai/blog/stop-using-commas-in-emails
    - Chelsea Castle, "Cold Email 101", 23 March 2023: https://www.lavender.ai/blog/cold-email-101
  - Gong, "cold email stats", Jonathan Costet, 20 October 2021: https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-email-stats
- Findings:
  - Boomerang: emails of 50 to 125 words got response rates above 50%. Emails written at a 3rd-grade reading level got 53% responses, against 39% at college level (a 36% lift). Subject lines of 3 to 4 words got the most responses. Emails with 1 to 3 questions were 50% more likely to get a reply than emails with none. Slightly warm or slightly negative wording beat completely neutral wording by 10 to 15%.
  - Lavender: cold emails of 25 to 50 words reply best. Emails written at a 3rd to 5th-grade reading level get "67% more replies"; Lavender does not publish how it measured this. Their rule: "One idea per email. One idea per paragraph. One idea per sentence." They recommend one-line sentences or paragraphs of 1 to 2 sentences, because most emails are first opened on a phone.
  - Gong: longer emails of 30 to 150 words are "significantly more effective in booking a meeting" if they are personalized and every sentence has a purpose. "Hope all is well" is linked to 24% more meetings booked. Gong measured meetings booked, not replies.
  - Not verified: Gong figures often cited elsewhere ("under 100 words", "3 to 4 sentences", "follow-ups of 4+ sentences get 15x more meetings"). I could not find them on a Gong page I could read.
  - Caveat for French: grade levels come from English readability formulas and don't transfer to French as numbers. What carries over is the direction: common words, short sentences.
  - What the three agree on: short (roughly 50 to 125 words for a normal email), plain words, one idea at a time. The exact word counts disagree.

## 12. Isabelle Canivet: *Bien rédiger pour le Web* (in French)

- Sources: *Bien rédiger pour le Web*, Eyrolles, several editions; 5th edition 23 September 2021 (670 to 688 pages depending on the listing). The first edition's year was not verified. I read the 5th edition's table of contents, a PDF published by her firm Yellow Dolphins: https://yellowdolphins.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/bien-rediger-pour-le-web-table-des-matieres-5e-edition-isabelle-canivet.pdf. I also read the dossier « La pyramide inversée », co-signed by Isabelle and Jean-Marc (undated): https://yellowdolphins.com/publications/dossier-la-pyramide-inversee/. Her old site action-redaction.com now redirects to an unrelated gambling site, so don't cite it.
- Rules, taken from her own chapter titles:
  - Go straight to the point: « Une règle d'or : aller à l'essentiel ». « Pas d'introduction sans intérêt ». Inverted pyramid, with the key message repeated in the chapô.
  - Make the text understandable: avoid jargon, explain acronyms, choose the right register. Use language everyone can read: « Humour, pas de gaffe ! », no anglicisms or regionalisms, absolute time references.
  - Keep it concise: she has sections on the length of sentences and of paragraphs, plus « Un texte sans redondances » and « Soyez clair ! ».
  - Keep the language concrete: « Éviter le langage creux et les effets de mode » and « Chiffres à l'appui ! ».
  - Keep it consistent and reliable: consistent labels and layout, correct spelling and grammar.
  - From the dossier: « Placer une information importante trop loin dans le texte risque de totalement échapper à la moitié des gens. »
  - Not verified: the sentence-length figure she gives in the book. A secondary source says 15 to 20 words on average.

## 13. Jean-Marc Hardy: « Checklist du rédacteur web » (in French)

- Sources: « 44 conseils pour bien écrire pour le Web », Yellow Dolphins, created 2001 and updated December 2015: https://yellowdolphins.com/publications/articles/checklist-pour-bien-ecrire-sur-le-web/ (read in full). « Les techniques journalistiques en ligne » (undated): https://yellowdolphins.com/publications/articles/les-techniques-journalistiques-en-ligne/ (read). He also wrote *Rédaction & neurosciences* (Eyrolles, July 2022). Hardy is Canivet's business partner at Yellow Dolphins.
- Rules:
  - « Rédigez des phrases courtes : Moins de 20 mots si possible. » Each sentence centered on one key idea.
  - Between two equivalent expressions, pick the shorter one (« sauf » rather than « à l'exception de »). Prefer the active voice. Split a relative clause into two sentences. Don't pile figures, acronyms and asides into one sentence.
  - No legal, administrative or sales jargon unless the audience uses it, and no acronyms that are not universal.
  - One idea per paragraph, stated in its first sentence.
  - Adapt the tone to the reader and keep it the same throughout: don't switch between impersonal and personal.
  - Go straight to the point: no ceremonial opening and no hedging. Titles of 4 to 10 words, explicit, with no wordplay, no exclamation marks and no exaggerated promotional emphasis. Chapô of 50 words at most. Use absolute dates (« en décembre 2017 ») rather than « le mois prochain ». « Chaque faute d'orthographe vous discrédite. »

## 14. Sélim Niederhoffer: *Le guide du copywriting* (in French)

- Sources: *Le guide du copywriting*, Eyrolles, 1st ed. 25 March 2021, 2nd ed. November 2025. His blog Les Mots Magiques:
  - « Pourquoi on interdit la voix passive en copywriting ? », 24 March 2026: https://www.les-mots-magiques.com/ecrire-mieux-vendre-plus/voix-passive-copywriting (read)
  - « Réécriture : les 5 conseils indispensables », 5 June 2024: https://www.les-mots-magiques.com/redaction-web/reecriture-textes (read)
- Rules:
  - « Votre lecteur doit comprendre votre phrase du premier coup. »
  - Use the active voice and say who does what, for whom, and how. The passive voice « enlève de l'énergie à votre texte ».
  - Put a person in the text: « on achète plus facilement à un humain qu'à une structure impersonnelle ».
  - Write first and rewrite later. Reread each sentence, try another version of it, and keep the better one.
  - « Réécrire ça veut très souvent dire couper et supprimer. »

## Where they agree

| Shared rule | Who says it |
|---|---|
| Write the way a person talks, and sound like a real person rather than an organization | Ogilvy, Graham, Fried, Dry, Welsh, Zinsser (be yourself), Handley ("you" and "us"), Niederhoffer (a human, not a structure) |
| Short words, short sentences, short paragraphs | Ogilvy, Zinsser, Graham, Hardy (<20 words), Canivet, Dry, Welsh, Lavender, Boomerang |

| No jargon, buzzwords or stock business phrases | Ogilvy, Fried, Braun, Handley, Dry, Hardy, Canivet, Lavender |
| Concrete and checkable (a figure, a detail) rather than adjectives | Dry, Braun, Wiebe, Canivet, NNg (objective), Fried, Handley |
| Don't exaggerate; no promotional emphasis | NNg (marketese), Dry, Hardy (titles) |
| Active voice: say who does what | Zinsser, Niederhoffer, Hardy, Dry |

| Don't try to impress | Graham, Ogilvy, Dry, Zinsser |

## Where they disagree, or a rule fits only one genre

- **"Write like you talk" or "write like your customer talks".** Braun rejects the first for cold emails. The two fit together: talk like a person, but use the customer's words. For Rivelec, the reader's trade vocabulary is welcome, marketing vocabulary is not. Braun and Hardy both allow jargon the audience itself uses.
- **Em dash.** Handley defends it and says it is not a machine sign. The current `rules.md` bans it. My judgment, not a finding: French business mail rarely uses the tiret cadratin, so the ban costs nothing in French. It is a choice about how the text will be perceived, not a quality rule backed by these sources.
- **Grammar and register.** Dry's "don't worry about grammar" and contractions are informal English. Hardy and Canivet say the opposite for French (« Chaque faute d'orthographe vous discrédite »). Keep spelling correct and keep *vouvoiement*; conversational ≠ sloppy.
- **Polite set phrases.** Zinsser and Braun cut filler, and Hardy bans ceremonial openings on web pages. Yet Gong found "Hope all is well" linked to 24% more meetings booked. Conclusion: in an email, one usual set phrase is fine; on a web page, it is not.
- **Humor and attitude.** Fried praises Woot and Saddleback for their cheek. Canivet warns « Humour, pas de gaffe ! » and asks for language every reader can understand. For industrial customers, use humor rarely.
- **Opinion or objectivity.** Boomerang: emails with some warmth or opinion get more replies. NNg: web pages should be objective. The first is mail, the second is web pages.
- **Email length.** Lavender says 25 to 50 words (cold), Braun 74 to 100, Boomerang 50 to 125 (all email), and Gong 30 to 150 for meetings. These are cold-email figures. A reply to a customer's request can be longer, but should still be short.
- **Genre-specific rules.** One-line paragraphs are for LinkedIn and mail on a phone (Welsh, Lavender), not for articles. Chapô, subheadings and inverted pyramid are for web pages and articles (Canivet, Hardy, NNg). Dry's three tests and the ban on marketese matter most on product pages.

## Rules for Gemini

Short rules in French for `rules.md` or the prompt. Tags show where each one comes from.

1. Écris comme tu le dirais à un client que tu respectes, en face de toi ; une phrase qui ne se dirait pas à l'oral se réécrit. [Ogilvy] [Graham] [Fried] [Dry] [Welsh] [Niederhoffer]
2. Reprends les mots du client et de son métier, pas ceux de la plaquette ; le vocabulaire technique qu'il emploie lui-même est permis. [Braun] [Fried] [Dry] [Wiebe] [Hardy]
3. Préfère le mot courant et court ; entre deux tournures équivalentes, prends la plus courte (« sauf » plutôt que « à l'exception de », « maintenant » plutôt que « à l'heure actuelle »). [Ogilvy] [Zinsser] [Graham] [Hardy] [Welsh]
4. Fais des phrases courtes, une idée par phrase, moins de 20 mots le plus souvent, sans les rendre toutes de la même longueur. [Ogilvy] [Zinsser] [Hardy] [Dry] [Wiebe] [Data]
5. Un paragraphe, une idée, annoncée dès sa première phrase. [Hardy] [Canivet] [NNg] [Data]
6. Commence par l'essentiel : pas de préambule, pas de mise en contexte que le lecteur connaît déjà. [Canivet] [Hardy] [NNg] [Graham]
7. Écris à la voix active, avec un sujet qui agit : on doit savoir qui fait quoi. [Zinsser] [Niederhoffer] [Hardy] [Dry]
8. Pas de jargon commercial ni de sigle non expliqué. [Ogilvy] [Fried] [Braun] [Handley] [Hardy] [Canivet]
9. Pas de formule que n'importe quel concurrent pourrait signer (« solution globale », « leader », « innovant », « sur mesure ») : si tout le monde peut l'écrire, elle ne dit rien. [Fried] [Dry] [Braun] [Canivet]
10. Remplace l'adjectif par un fait qu'on peut voir ou vérifier : un chiffre du brief, une pièce, une étape. [Dry] [Braun] [Wiebe] [Canivet] [NNg]
11. N'exagère pas : pas de superlatif, pas d'emphase publicitaire, pas de point d'exclamation. [NNg] [Dry] [Hardy]
12. Coupe ce qui ne sert pas : adverbe qui répète le verbe, « vraiment », « juste », « un peu », phrase qui redit la précédente. [Zinsser] [Braun] [Graham] [Welsh] [Niederhoffer]
13. Pour chaque phrase qui vante quelque chose, demande-toi « et alors ? » et écris ce que ça change pour le lecteur. [Wiebe] [Handley] [Dry]
14. Parle en personne, en « je » ou « nous » à « vous », pas en entreprise impersonnelle ; garde le même ton du début à la fin. [Zinsser] [Handley] [Niederhoffer] [Hardy]
15. Ne cherche pas à impressionner : pas de mot rare ni de tournure savante là où un mot simple suffit. [Graham] [Ogilvy] [Dry] [Zinsser]
16. Suppose que le lecteur ne connaît pas le sujet mais qu'il n'est pas bête : explique sans insister. [Handley] [Graham]
17. Orthographe et grammaire irréprochables ; un ton simple n'est pas un ton relâché. [Hardy] [Canivet]
18. Relis chaque phrase comme si tu la disais, et coupe ou réécris celles qui accrochent. [Ogilvy] [Graham] [Dry] [Braun] [Niederhoffer]

## Per genre

### Mail
1. Vise 50 à 125 mots ; au-delà, chaque phrase doit avoir une raison d'être. [Data] [Braun]
2. Objet court et explicite, quelques mots (Boomerang : 3 à 4 mots en anglais ; en français, 3 à 5 est l'équivalent le plus proche). [Data]
3. Des paragraphes d'une ou deux phrases : le mail sera souvent lu sur un téléphone. [Data] [Welsh]
4. Dis clairement ce que tu attends du lecteur, par une question simple à laquelle il peut répondre oui ou non. [Ogilvy] [Braun] [Data]
5. Une formule de politesse habituelle en ouverture ou en fin est bienvenue ; pas de relance qui quémande (« avez-vous bien reçu mon mail ? », « je voulais juste savoir si… »). [Data] [Braun]

### Article de blog
1. Titre de 4 à 10 mots, explicite, sans jeu de mots ni point d'exclamation. [Hardy]
2. Chapô de 50 mots au plus, qui donne l'essentiel ; le reste de l'article détaille. [Hardy] [Canivet] [NNg]
3. Des intertitres qui disent ce que contient la section, et des listes pour les énumérations. [Hardy] [NNg] [Canivet]
4. Des dates absolues (« en mars 2026 ») plutôt que « récemment » ou « le mois prochain ». [Hardy] [Canivet]
5. Dis au lecteur quelque chose de neuf et d'utile dès l'introduction, qui ne tourne pas autour du sujet. [Graham] [Canivet]

### Page produit
1. Ton factuel : ce que fait le produit, sur quoi, avec quelles valeurs ; pas de « marketese ». [NNg] [Fried]
2. Chaque avantage passe les trois tests : on peut le voir, le vérifier, et un concurrent ne pourrait pas l'écrire tel quel. [Dry]
3. Chaque affirmation s'appuie sur un fait du brief (valeur, norme, cas client) ; sans preuve, on ne l'écrit pas. [Wiebe] [Canivet]
4. Libellés et boutons courts, dans les mots du client, sans terme interne. [Fried]
5. Des phrases où le produit (ou l'utilisateur) agit : sujet, verbe, résultat, plutôt que des tournures passives ou des « permet de » enchaînés. [Niederhoffer] [Zinsser]

### Post LinkedIn
1. Première ligne courte, qui donne envie de lire la suite sans promettre plus que le post. [Welsh] [Dry]
2. Écrit pour le téléphone : blocs courts et espace blanc, mais pas de retour à la ligne au milieu d'une phrase. [Welsh]
3. Pars d'un moment réel (une pièce, une mesure, une question d'un client) plutôt que d'un thème général. [Handley]
4. Parle au lecteur en « vous », avec des mots simples, et coupe 30 % du premier jet. [Welsh]
5. Si le post est long, termine par un rappel d'une phrase de l'idée principale, sans morale. [Welsh]

## Not verified from a primary source

- Harry Dry's three rules: I read them through David Perell's summary and clips of the recorded interview, not on a page Dry wrote. `marketingexamples.com/copywriting/three-rules` returned HTTP 500.
- Ann Handley's "25 words per sentence" limit and "active voice" rule appear in secondary summaries of *Everybody Writes*. I did not find them in her own texts, so I left them out.

- Gong: "under 100 words", "3 to 4 sentences", "follow-ups of 4+ sentences get 15x more meetings". These are cited by Lavender and secondary sites, but I found none of them on a Gong page.
- Lavender's "67% more replies at a 3rd to 5th-grade reading level": this is on Lavender's own blog, but no method is published. Treat it as a vendor claim.
- Getting Real's publication year (2006) comes from memory; the book's pages don't state it. *Bien rédiger pour le Web*: the first edition's year was not checked.
- Ogilvy's memo is dated 7 September 1982 by most copies, but Boing Boing says 1981. The text is the same in both.
